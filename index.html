Cancer genetics and therapeutics

How cancer cells end their RNAs, and how to use it against them.

We study the genetics of cancer and look for new ways to treat it. Our projects range from alternative polyadenylation in patient tumors to combination therapies with transcription termination inhibitors and the anticancer activity of local plant extracts.

Research

What we work on

We combine analysis of large public patient datasets with laboratory testing. Current projects include the three below.

APApoly(A) site choice

Alternative polyadenylation dysregulation in cancer

Most human genes have more than one site where their transcript can be cut and given a poly(A) tail. Tumors often shift toward proximal sites, making shorter 3′ UTRs that escape microRNA control. We map these shifts across large patient cohorts and ask which changes in the cleavage and polyadenylation machinery drive them. In a recent pan-cancer study, amplification of CPSF1, which often occurs together with MYC amplification, was linked to 3′ UTR changes and poorer survival even in tumors where MYC was not amplified.

PaperAlahmari, Biology 2025

TerminationPol II readthrough

Combination therapies with transcription termination inhibitors

When the cleavage machinery cuts a new transcript at its poly(A) site, it also signals RNA polymerase II to stop. Blocking this step leaves the polymerase reading past the ends of genes and disrupts many transcripts at once. We test which anticancer drugs work better when combined with transcription termination inhibitors, looking for combinations that are more effective together than either drug alone.

Plantsnatural products

Anticancer activity of local plant extracts

Many plants that grow in Saudi Arabia are used in traditional medicine. We test extracts from local plants for activity against cancer cells, to find which ones deserve closer study and which compounds are responsible for their effects.
